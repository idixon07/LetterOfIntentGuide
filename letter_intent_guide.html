Part 1: Pre-Writing Foundation

Mini-Lesson: Understanding Purpose and Audience

Objective: Students identify what makes letters of intent different from other writing.

Activities:

  1. Comparison Exercise - Provide examples of a personal essay, cover letter, and letter of intent. Have students identify differences in purpose, tone, and content.
  2. Audience Analysis - Students research their target program/opportunity and create an "audience profile" answering:
    • What does this organization value?
    • What problems do they solve?
    • What qualities are they seeking?
    • What language/terminology do they use?

Mini-Lesson: The "So What?" Test

Objective: Students learn to move beyond generic statements to meaningful claims.

Activities:

  1. Weak vs. Strong Statements - Analyze pairs like:

    Weak: "I am a hard worker."

    Strong: "Last semester, I balanced a part-time job with AP courses by creating a detailed study schedule, which helped me maintain a 3.8 GPA while working 15 hours weekly."

  2. The Three-Level Depth Exercise:
    • Level 1: Generic claim ("I'm passionate about engineering")
    • Level 2: Add specificity ("I'm passionate about sustainable engineering")
    • Level 3: Add evidence ("After designing a rainwater collection system for my school's garden that reduced water costs by 30%, I became committed to sustainable engineering solutions")

Part 2: Structural Framework

The Opening Paragraph Formula

Teach students this four-sentence structure:

  1. Hook: Compelling opening that shows enthusiasm or relevant experience
  2. Purpose: Clear statement of what they're applying for
  3. Qualification Preview: Brief mention of their strongest credential
  4. Transition: Lead into the body

Example Template:

"When [specific experience], I discovered [insight about field/opportunity]. I am writing to express my strong interest in [specific program/position] at [institution]. My experience in [relevant area] and commitment to [relevant goal] have prepared me to contribute meaningfully to [program]. I believe my background in [area] aligns well with [program's focus]."

The Body Paragraph Structure

Teach the P.E.E. Model:

  • Point: Topic sentence stating the qualification/experience
  • Evidence: Specific example with concrete details
  • Explanation: Connect the evidence to the opportunity (the "so what?")

Student Checklist for Each Body Paragraph:

  • ☐ Does it start with a clear claim about my qualifications?
  • ☐ Have I included specific names, numbers, or details?
  • ☐ Have I explained WHY this experience matters for this opportunity?
  • ☐ Does it connect to something the program values?

The Closing Paragraph

Students should include:

  1. Brief summary of fit
  2. Forward-looking statement about contribution
  3. Gratitude and call to action
  4. Professional sign-off

Part 3: Writing Workshop Activities

Activity 1: Evidence Scavenger Hunt

Time: 20 minutes

Students create a "qualifications inventory":

  • Academic achievements (grades, awards, challenging courses)
  • Extracurricular involvement (leadership roles, commitment duration)
  • Work/volunteer experience (skills gained, impact made)
  • Personal projects (initiative, creativity, results)
  • Skills (technical, linguistic, interpersonal)

Have them write at least 3 specific details for each category.

Activity 2: The Specificity Challenge

Time: 15 minutes

Give students generic sentences and have them compete to make them most specific:

Generic: "I volunteered at a hospital"

Improved: "I volunteered 100 hours in the pediatric unit at Memorial Hospital, where I assisted nurses with patient activities and created a children's book lending library"

Activity 3: Peer Audience Test

Time: 30 minutes

Process:

  1. Students exchange drafts (without sharing what opportunity they're applying for)
  2. Reader identifies: What is the student applying for? What are their top 3 qualifications? What makes them stand out?
  3. Writer reveals the opportunity and compares reader's takeaways with their intentions
  4. Discussion: Where did clarity break down? What needs emphasis?

Activity 4: Tone Tuning

Time: 20 minutes

Provide the same content written in three tones (too casual, too stiff, just right). Students identify which is appropriate and why. Then they revise a paragraph from their own letter, checking for:

  • Professional but authentic voice
  • Confidence without arrogance
  • Enthusiasm without desperation

Part 4: Revision Strategies

Teacher Conference Guide

Use these questions during individual conferences:

Content Questions:

  • "Can you tell me in one sentence why you're the right fit? Does your letter say that clearly?"
  • "What's your strongest qualification? Where does that appear in your letter?"
  • "What does the reader learn about you that they couldn't get from your transcript?"

Evidence Questions:

  • "You mention [vague claim]. Can you tell me a specific story that shows this?"
  • "What results came from this experience?"
  • "How does this experience connect to what you want to do?"

Technical Questions:

  • "Read your opening sentence aloud. Does it grab attention?"
  • "Where is your letter most clear? Where does it get fuzzy?"

Self-Revision Checklist for Students

Content & Purpose:

  • ☐ Opening paragraph clearly states what I'm applying for
  • ☐ I can identify my thesis/main argument
  • ☐ Each paragraph has a clear purpose
  • ☐ I've included specific examples (names, numbers, outcomes)
  • ☐ I explain HOW my experiences prepare me for this opportunity
  • ☐ I demonstrate knowledge of the program/organization

Voice & Tone:

  • ☐ The letter sounds like me (authentic voice)
  • ☐ Tone is professional but not robotic
  • ☐ I show enthusiasm without sounding desperate
  • ☐ I'm confident without being arrogant

Technical Execution:

  • ☐ No spelling or grammar errors
  • ☐ Consistent verb tense throughout
  • ☐ Varied sentence structure
  • ☐ Appropriate length (usually 1-1.5 pages)
  • ☐ Professional formatting with proper heading
  • ☐ Strong topic sentences in each paragraph

Part 5: Common Issues & Solutions

Issue: Generic, Template-Like Writing

Symptoms: Could apply to any program, no specific details, sounds like AI

Solutions:

  • Require students to include 3 specific facts about the program they researched
  • Ban phrases like "I am writing to express my interest" in favor of stronger openings
  • Implement the "Find & Replace Test": If you can replace the program name with any other program and the letter still works, it's too generic

Issue: Listing Achievements Without Context

Symptoms: Reads like a resume in paragraph form, no explanation of significance

Solutions:

  • Teach the "So What? Then What?" follow-up: After every achievement, students must answer why it matters and what they learned
  • Require transition phrases: "This experience taught me...", "As a result...", "Building on this..."

Issue: Weak or Buried Thesis

Symptoms: Reader can't identify the main argument, unclear what student wants

Solutions:

  • Have students write their thesis on a sticky note and place it on their draft—if they can't articulate it clearly, neither can the reader
  • Practice the "elevator pitch": Students explain their fit in 30 seconds before writing

Issue: Poor Evidence Selection

Symptoms: Irrelevant stories, outdated achievements, no connection to opportunity

Solutions:

  • Create evidence relevance scoring: For each example, rate 1-5 how relevant it is to the specific opportunity
  • Quality over quantity: Better to deeply explore 2-3 strong examples than superficially mention 6

Issue: Mechanical Errors Undermine Professionalism

Symptoms: Spelling mistakes, wrong names, inconsistent formatting

Solutions:

  • Require printed drafts (errors show up better on paper)
  • Peer proofreading protocol: Reader circles every error but doesn't correct it—writer must identify and fix
  • Create program-specific checklist (correct program name, department, contact person)

Part 6: Differentiation Strategies

For Struggling Writers:

  • Provide sentence frames and transitional phrases
  • Use the "talk-then-write" method: Record students explaining their qualifications, then transcribe and refine
  • Break the letter into smaller assignments (opening only, one body paragraph at a time)
  • Offer model letters with annotations explaining what makes them effective

For Advanced Writers:

  • Challenge them to eliminate all weak verbs and generic adjectives
  • Have them write multiple openings and select the strongest
  • Encourage sophisticated connecting of multiple experiences into a coherent narrative
  • Ask them to mentor peers and explain their revision decisions

For English Language Learners:

  • Focus first on content and organization, then refine language
  • Provide vocabulary banks of professional terms and transitional phrases
  • Allow extra time for language polishing
  • Pair with native speakers for tone/idiom checking

Part 7: Assessment Tools

Holistic Rubric (Recommended)

Distinguished (4):

  • Compelling opening immediately establishes purpose and unique fit
  • Sophisticated integration of specific evidence that clearly connects to opportunity
  • Authentic voice balances professionalism with personality
  • Flawless mechanics and professional presentation
  • Demonstrates deep research and understanding of program/organization

Proficient (3):

  • Clear purpose statement and organizational structure
  • Adequate specific evidence supporting qualifications
  • Appropriate professional tone throughout
  • Minor mechanical errors that don't distract
  • Shows basic knowledge of opportunity

Developing (2):

  • Purpose present but not immediately clear
  • Some specific evidence but relies heavily on generic statements
  • Inconsistent tone (too casual or too stiff)
  • Mechanical errors occasionally interfere with meaning
  • Limited demonstration of program knowledge

Beginning (1):

  • Unclear purpose or misunderstanding of letter format
  • Primarily generic claims without supporting evidence
  • Inappropriate tone or voice
  • Frequent mechanical errors obscure meaning
  • No evidence of research into opportunity

Analytical Rubric Categories

(4-point scale for each):

  1. Clarity of Purpose & Audience Awareness
  2. Quality & Relevance of Evidence
  3. Organization & Structure
  4. Voice, Tone & Authenticity
  5. Mechanics & Professional Presentation

Part 8: Extension & Enrichment

Real-World Connection Activities

Guest Speakers

Invite admissions officers, hiring managers, or program directors to discuss what they look for in letters of intent

Mock Selection Committee

Students anonymize their letters and serve on committees to "admit" candidates, then discuss selection criteria

Professional Review

Connect with community professionals willing to provide feedback on student letters

Multi-Draft Portfolio

Students maintain all drafts showing evolution from brainstorming through final product

Quick Reference Guide

Week-by-Week Implementation

  • Week 1: Purpose, audience analysis, and evidence gathering
  • Week 2: Structural framework and drafting opening paragraphs
  • Week 3: Body paragraph development with P.E.E. model
  • Week 4: Closing paragraphs and first complete drafts
  • Week 5: Peer review and teacher conferences
  • Week 6: Revision, proofreading, and final submission

Sentence Starters Students Can Use

  • "My interest in [field] began when..."
  • "Through my experience with [activity], I developed..."
  • "This opportunity aligns with my goal to..."
  • "I am particularly drawn to [specific program feature] because..."
  • "My work in [area] has prepared me to..."

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Opening with "My name is..." (usually unnecessary)
  • "I am writing to express my interest in..." (weak opening)
  • "Since I was a child..." (often feels forced unless genuinely relevant)
  • Repeated "I" sentence starts (monotonous structure)
  • Unexplained jargon or acronyms
  • Apologetic language ("I know I'm not the most qualified, but...")
  • Demands or presumptions ("When I am accepted..." vs. "If selected...")

Parent Communication Tip

Send home a one-page guide explaining the assignment's purpose and how parents can help (brainstorming experiences, proofreading) without taking over the writing process.

Remember: The goal is not perfection but authentic, professional communication that genuinely represents each student's unique qualifications and aspirations.
